Someone searching "make an ebook in Canva" finds tutorials showing a 20-page lead magnet built from a template in an afternoon. Someone searching "AI book generator" finds InkMagnet promising a researched book from a single topic. It's tempting to treat the two as competitors on the same shortlist, because both end in a finished-looking PDF. They don't do the same job. Canva is a design tool with an AI assistant bolted onto a blank canvas: you supply the words, the outline and the editing time, and it makes the page look professional. InkMagnet supplies the words too, researched and reviewed, structured into chapters you never had to write. Mixing the two up is how someone with a topic and no manuscript pays for a design subscription that was never built to write anything, and how someone with a stack of slides to prettify pays per book for research they didn't need.

## What Canva actually gives you

Canva's ebook and workbook templates, thousands of them across the Free and Pro tiers, are genuinely well designed: consistent grids, matching palettes, drag-and-drop layout. You open one, swap the placeholder headings and body copy for your own, drop in your own images, and export a PDF. That's a fast, real workflow for a 10-to-20 page lead magnet or workbook when the content already lives in your head or an old blog post. Canva Docs extends the same idea to longer writing inside the platform, and Magic Write, its AI assistant, can draft a paragraph, a heading or an outline on request, on Free, Pro and Business plans alike, in 20 languages.

None of that changes the starting assumption: you bring the content. Canva's own documentation frames Magic Write as a way to draft or rewrite text you already have in mind, not a research tool. It doesn't browse the live web, cite a source, or hold context across sixty pages. Independent testing through 2026 puts its practical ceiling at roughly 1,500 words in and 2,000 words out per generation, fine for a caption or a short section, nowhere near a nonfiction chapter, let alone a 30,000-word book planned as one structure.

## Magic Write: a drafting nudge, not a writer

Ask Magic Write to expand a bullet into a paragraph and it will, fluently. Ask it to write chapter four of a book about supply chain management and it has no chapter one to stay consistent with, no memory of what chapter two already covered, and no way to check a claim against a real source before committing to a sentence. Reviewers testing it through 2026 keep landing on the same verdict: good for a first draft or a caption, not reliable for anything that needs to be factually accurate, because fixing grammar isn't the same as fact-checking a name, a date or a price. That's a fine trade-off for a marketing blurb. It's a real problem for a chapter claiming a market "grew 34% last year" that nobody checked.

InkMagnet runs the research step first, not as an add-on. You describe a topic, the pipeline searches the live web, reads full source pages instead of snippets, plans the chapter structure before any prose exists, drafts each chapter against what it found, and runs an automated review pass that checks chapters against each other for repetition and drift. [The same research-first order is the reason most AI-written books read thin in the first place](/blog/ai-books-read-like-blog-posts/): nothing gets written until there's something real to write from.

## Pricing: per seat, every month, versus once per book

Canva's free plan covers basic templates and roughly 200 monthly "standard" AI actions, Magic Write included, shared across every AI feature you touch. Canva Pro runs $18 a month or $144 billed annually, for one person, and raises that shared AI allowance to about 500 actions a month, still split between text, image generation and every other Magic Studio tool competing for the same pool. Add a second person and you're into Canva Business, priced per seat at roughly $25 a month, $250 a year. None of those tiers include research, fact-checking or a typesetting engine; they buy templates and a shared credit meter that resets every 30 days whether you used it or not.

InkMagnet charges once per book, scaled to length: $9.99 for 30–45 pages up to $34.99 for a 161–200 page book. Research, writing, an automated review pass, chapter illustrations, a designed cover, and both PDF and EPUB export sit inside that single number, with full commercial rights and unlimited edits and recompiles included. Nothing runs in a month you don't touch it, and there's no shared credit pool split between the words and the pictures.

## The gap between a Canva PDF and an actual ebook

Even after a template is filled in, what comes out of Canva is a PDF, not a file Amazon KDP or an ebook store will take directly as a reflowable ebook. Getting from a Canva-exported PDF to a valid EPUB or MOBI still means running it through a separate converter such as Calibre, checking the reflow didn't break the layout, and rebuilding a table of contents Canva doesn't generate for you. That's a manageable extra step for a print-only workbook. It's a second project for anyone planning an actual Kindle edition. InkMagnet exports a validated EPUB alongside the print-ready PDF from the same generation, because the compiler treats both formats as outputs of one typeset book from the start.

## Where Canva genuinely wins

None of this makes Canva a bad tool, it's the wrong tool for a specific job. If the content already exists, a course outline, a set of slides, a client's brand guidelines, and the deliverable is a short, polished lead magnet or workbook under 20 pages, Canva's template library and drag-and-drop editor gets there faster than any AI writing pipeline, [and a short lead magnet is often exactly what a landing page needs](/blog/ebook-as-your-best-lead-magnet/). Its design range for slides, social graphics and one-off print pieces has no real equivalent in InkMagnet, which is built to write and typeset a book, not to be a general design tool.

But a template stays blank until someone writes in it.
