Self-paced courses on the big marketplaces finish below 15% of the time, and independent platforms don't do dramatically better at 30-50%, according to [Ruzuku's 2026 completion-rate benchmarks](https://www.ruzuku.com/learn/articles/course-completion-rates). Half of everyone who drops out does it in the first two weeks, usually citing lack of time (38%) or lost motivation (25%) rather than the material being wrong for them, per [Skillademia's 2026 completion statistics](https://www.skillademia.com/statistics/online-course-completion-statistics/). A video lesson that ends with "now go apply this" and nothing else to hold onto is asking students to build their own structure at exactly the point they're most likely to quit.

## Why a PDF outperforms a "resources" tab

Course platforms already let you attach a resources tab, a linked Google Doc or a slide deck export next to each module. None of it survives the way a bound workbook does. A tab full of loose links gets abandoned the moment a student closes the browser tab; a Google Doc invites nobody to write in the margins. A designed companion book someone downloads once behaves like the printed workbooks bundled with cohort-based bootcamps, which post completion rates in the 72-90% range compared to a self-paced course sitting alone in a library. The book isn't doing the teaching. It's giving the student a physical container for the 38% of dropouts who say they simply ran out of structured time, by turning "watch six videos this week" into a checklist with a page number.

Sellers on the workbook side of this market already treat the companion as the product, not the freebie: a $29 workbook next to a free cheat sheet, with the video course itself as the $199 upsell. A course creator selling the workbook and the video separately is really selling the same throughline twice, which is the strongest argument for shipping them together as one thing a student pays for once and keeps.

## What makes a companion book different from a slide export

Three structural choices separate a workbook students finish from a PDF that hits `download` and stays in the folder:

- **Continuous numbering across the whole book, not per-module resets.** InkMagnet's typesetting engine supports an "items" numbering mode built for exactly this: every exercise, worksheet or reflection prompt gets a running number (Exercise 1, Exercise 2, Exercise 3) that keeps counting through chapter breaks instead of resetting to 1 at the start of each module. You can set the label yourself (Exercise, Worksheet, Prompt) from the book's numbering settings after generation, and it stays consistent whether the student is on module 2 or module 9.
- **A structure a student can skim before committing.** The book ships with a clickable table of contents and a chapter-opening page for each module, so someone previewing the workbook on a sales page can see the shape of eight weeks of curriculum in one scroll, not just a course outline copied into a PDF.
- **Room to actually write.** A workbook that's just paragraphs restating the video script gives a student nothing to do with a pen. Numbered prompts and worksheets need blank space built into the layout, which is a typesetting decision, not something a Word export handles by default.
