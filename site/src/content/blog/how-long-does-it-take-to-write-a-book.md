Two numbers circle every "how long does it take to write a book" search, and they contradict each other. One camp advertises a finished ebook "in 2 minutes." The other, from people who actually write books for a living, lands on ten months. Both are honest. They're just answering different questions, and the gap between them is really a question of what "finished" includes. A useful comparison has to ask what clock is actually running, not just how long it reads.

## Writing it yourself: 6 to 12 months, if it ships at all

Reedsy's studio survey of working authors puts the typical span at six months to a year for a full manuscript, and that's for people who finish. Nonfiction tends to move faster than fiction because there's no plot to sustain, but a 40,000-70,000 word draft still commonly runs half a year once research, several rewrite passes and the inevitable stretches where the project stalls are counted in. None of that includes editing, formatting or a cover, which is a separate project on a separate timeline once the manuscript exists. The honest cost of the DIY route isn't money. It's the fact that the deadline is whenever you personally get back to chapter six.

## A ghostwriter: a median of ten months, and the clock stops at the manuscript

Gotham Ghostwriters' Business Book ROI Study, one of the more rigorously sourced looks at the industry, puts the median author time at ten months to write a book with a ghostwriter, with traditionally published business authors averaging around twelve and self-published ones closer to six. Other ghostwriting agencies quote a wider band of three months to a year depending on length and how fast you return interview notes and feedback. What that number buys is real craft: a writer who interviews you, drafts, revises and defends the manuscript's structure. What it doesn't include is formatting or a cover. [What a ghostwriter's fee actually covers](/blog/ghostwriter-cost-what-you-get/) breaks down the money side, but the time side matters just as much: once the manuscript is delivered, you're back to square one on turning a Word document into a book anyone can read on a Kindle or hold in print.

That next step isn't instant either. Professional formatters on marketplaces like Reedsy typically quote 5 to 14 business days for a standard nonfiction manuscript, per Kindlepreneur's own survey of the market, with rush jobs under a week costing a 25-50% premium. Add a cover designer's queue on top and a ghostwritten book routinely adds another one to three weeks after the ten-month writing clock already stopped.

## Traditional publishing: 18 to 24 months, and that's after the deal

A signed contract with a publisher starts an 18-to-24-month production clock (editing rounds, copyediting, cover design, catalog placement, printing) before a book reaches a shelf. That number doesn't include the part that comes first: querying agents or editors and waiting for an offer, which commonly adds another three to twelve months on its own. Stack the phases and a traditionally published nonfiction book is routinely a two-to-three-year project from the first pitch to a reader holding a copy, and the author doesn't control most of that timeline once the deal is signed.

## "In 2 minutes" tools: fast, because the writing already happened

This is where the other extreme comes from. Designrr's own homepage promises an ebook "in 2 minutes," and Sqribble's sales page demos a finished document in about a minute once you're clicking through templates. Both claims are true, and neither is about writing. [Our side-by-side with Designrr](/blog/inkmagnet-vs-designrr/) covers how it works: you import a blog archive, a transcript or a Word file you already wrote, and the software reflows it into a designed PDF. [The Sqribble comparison](/blog/inkmagnet-vs-sqribble/) covers the same gap from a different angle: fifty templates, and you supply every word yourself, typed or pasted in. The "minutes" clock on both tools starts after the actual writing is already done. It measures layout, not authorship, which is a fine trade if the words exist and all you need is a PDF, and a misleading one if you're comparing that number against a book that doesn't exist yet.

## Five ways to measure the same finish line

Lined up, the honest comparison looks like this: writing it yourself commonly runs six months to a year, assuming the project doesn't stall first. A ghostwriter's median is ten months for the manuscript alone, plus one to three more weeks once you add formatting and a cover. Traditional publishing is 18 to 24 months after a deal that itself took three to twelve months to land. A template tool like Designrr or Sqribble can produce a PDF in a couple of minutes, provided the manuscript inside it already exists somewhere else. An InkMagnet book, researched, written, illustrated, typeset and exported as PDF and EPUB, typically finishes in under an hour, because it's the only option on this list that does the writing and the production in the same run instead of treating them as two separate projects.

None of these methods is wrong for every case. A ghostwriter earns their ten months on a memoir that needs someone else's life story captured in their own voice, which isn't a job for a research pipeline. But for a lead magnet, a course companion or a nonfiction book where the value is the information rather than a ghostwritten voice, the honest question isn't "which tool is fastest."
