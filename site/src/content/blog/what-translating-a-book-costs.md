Ask a translation agency to turn a finished manuscript into a second language and the quote arrives as a separate line item, priced by the word, on top of whatever you already paid to have the book written. Ask InkMagnet to write the same book in English or in Polish and the price doesn't move at all. That's not a discount. It's because InkMagnet never translates a manuscript in the first place.

## What a human translator actually charges

Professional literary and nonfiction translation runs **$0.08 to $0.30 per word** depending on language pair, subject matter and whether the quote includes an editing pass, with specialized technical or legal content reaching $0.15-$0.50. Run that across a real manuscript and a 60,000-word nonfiction book, a common length for a retail-shelf title, lands at roughly **$5,000 to $12,000** for a quality human translation, stan na październik 2026.

Online translation platforms break that same range into tiers. Gengo prices its Standard tier at **$0.06/word** and its Advanced (native, proofread) tier at **$0.12/word**. GetBlend runs three tiers for the same language pair: Basic at **$0.087/word**, Professional (with editing) at **$0.13/word**, and Expert (subject-matter review included) at **$0.14/word**. The pattern across every one of these is the same: a cheap tier that skips the editorial pass, and a pricier one that doesn't, and the gap between them is exactly the proofing work [the languages piece](/blog/what-languages-does-inkmagnet-support/) already covers: a model or junior translator can produce grammatically clean text, but catching register, idiom and word order takes someone who reads the target language professionally.

## The cheap alternative has the same catch

Pure AI translation tools undercut all of that. BookTranslator.app advertises book translation from **$1.99**, claiming support for over 100 languages; TranslationAI prices a 50,000-word novel at **$2.50**; BookTranslate.ai lists roughly **$390 per book**. These numbers are real, and they're real options if budget is the only constraint. What they buy is a literal pass over the text, not a fluency check. A French how-to chapter translated this way reads grammatically fine and still carries the tell a native reader spots in the first paragraph: the wrong register, a "vous" where the genre expects something warmer, idiom that survived the trip from English a little too literally. It's the same gap between "the AI can produce the words" and "someone fluent confirmed they're the right words" that makes a ten-language drafting tool a different claim than a two-language, professionally proofed one.

## Credit-metered tools fold the cost into usage instead

AI drafting tools that bill by credits avoid a separate translation fee by burying the cost somewhere else. Designrr's Wordgenie spends roughly 12,000-20,000 credits on a typical ebook, with its own pricing noting that credit use varies by language, so the same topic can draw down more credits in one language than another. Extra credits then cost $17 one-time for 100,000, $57 one-time for a million, or $497 a year for a million a month. There's no line item that says "translation," but a book that quietly costs more credits in Polish than in English is still paying more for the same word count, just without a separate invoice to point at.

## A few services skip the upfront fee entirely

Not every option prices per word. Babelcube runs on a royalty-split model: a translator picks up your book and translates it for a cut of future sales instead of cash upfront, which removes the invoice but hands over part of every sale indefinitely. Reedsy instead matches you with a vetted translator for a flat project fee, closer to a traditional agency quote than a marketplace. Both are reasonable paths if you're commissioning a translation of a book you already wrote elsewhere, but neither changes the underlying math: translating a finished manuscript is priced, one way or another, as its own project, separate from writing it.

## Why InkMagnet's price doesn't move with language

InkMagnet's project form has two language options, English and Polish, and [the five pricing tiers](/blog/inkmagnet-pricing-tiers-page-count/) run $9.99 to $34.99 by page count alone. Pick Polish instead of English for the same 60-page topic and you pay the same $14.99 Standard price. That's not a translation discount, because nothing gets translated: InkMagnet researches, writes, edits and typesets the book directly in whichever language you chose, the same pipeline either way, the one already tuned for that language's hyphenation and editorial review. There's no second invoice for "localization" because there's no first manuscript being carried over from one language into the other.

That also means InkMagnet can't do what a translation service does: start from a manuscript you already have in English and hand back a Polish version of that exact text. If what you actually need is both languages of the same book, the honest path through InkMagnet is to run the topic through twice, once per language, and pay the tier price each time, say two Standard projects at $14.99 for $29.98 total. That's still a fraction of what a single 60,000-word professional translation costs on its own, and you end up with two manuscripts that were each researched, drafted and typeset for their language, not one original and one derivative.

## The one language gap this doesn't close

If a project genuinely needs a third language, French, German, Spanish and so on, InkMagnet doesn't write it, full stop, and no pricing tier covers that gap, as [the full language breakdown](/blog/what-languages-does-inkmagnet-support/) lays out. For that case, the professional and AI-translation rates above are the real comparison to make, applied to a finished English manuscript rather than commissioned from scratch, since a translated draft keeps the research and structure work already done, usually the more expensive half of getting a book right.

For a project that fits English or Polish, though, [start from your own topic](https://app.inkmagnet.com/auth/register) and the tier price is the whole bill, in either language, with no translation line waiting at the end.
