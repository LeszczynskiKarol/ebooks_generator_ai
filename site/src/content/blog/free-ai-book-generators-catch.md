Search "free AI book generator" and the promise looks identical across a dozen landing pages: no credit card, unlimited chapters, keep everything you write. Spend thirty minutes actually writing and the asterisks show up: a word cap that stops mid-chapter, an export button that's greyed out until you upgrade, or a model too weak to produce anything you'd publish under your name. Three questions cut through the marketing fast: does the export actually work on the free tier, is there a watermark on the finished file, and do you keep commercial rights. Most "free" tiers fail on the first one alone.

## What "free" actually gives you

[Squibler](https://www.squibler.io)'s free Limited Tier hands you **1,000 AI credits a month**, enough by the company's own account for one or two chapter drafts. Reaching the 10,000 credits a real book draft needs requires the **Plus plan at $29/month** ($16/month billed annually); unlimited credits sit behind **Pro at $89/month** ($49/month annually). A 20-chapter nonfiction manuscript on the free tier means waiting through roughly ten monthly credit resets before the draft even exists.

[Rytr](https://rytr.me/pricing)'s free plan caps out at **10,000 characters a month**, about 1,800 words, one short chapter, in a single language. Past that, Unlimited runs **$7.50 to $9/month** and Premium **$24 to $29/month** for API access and team seats. Neither Squibler's nor Rytr's free tier is built to finish a book. They're built to let you sample the writing quality before the meter starts.

[Sudowrite](https://sudowrite.com) is generous on trial credits, around 10,000, no card required, and its paid tiers run **$19 to $59/month** ($10 to $44/month annual) for 225,000 to 2 million credits. The catch sits somewhere else entirely: Sudowrite doesn't export to PDF, EPUB, or DOCX at all. Whatever tier you're on, you leave with raw text, not a book file. Formatting and export stay a separate problem the subscription never touches.

Some tools do clear the first bar. [Inkfluence AI](https://www.inkfluenceai.com) gives 5 chapters a month on its free plan, no watermark, and lets you keep commercial rights from day one, a genuinely rare combination. The trade-off is pace: 5 chapters a month means a 20-chapter book takes four months of resets if you write nothing else in that window, and the free tier still carries a small footer credit on your exports.

Credits rarely pay for a first draft alone, either. Regenerating a chapter that came out thin, or asking the model to expand a section that missed the point, draws from the same monthly pool as writing the next new chapter. On a 1,000-credit free tier, that's often enough for one clean pass through a chapter and nothing left for a second try, which is how a "free" draft ends up stretching across two or three billing cycles instead of one sitting.

## The part no free tier prices in

Clearing the credit cap doesn't mean you're holding a finished book. A raw AI-generated manuscript, even a good one, is still a wall of paragraphs with no typeset layout, no running heads, no table of contents, no cover. [Formatting it properly costs $147 to $250 for typesetting software or $200 to $1,000 for a freelance formatter](/blog/cost-to-create-an-ebook/), on top of whatever the AI subscription already charged. The free-tier hunt for a $0 draft routinely ends at a $200-plus formatting bill anyway, just a step later in the process.

There's also a compliance detail most free-tool marketing pages skip entirely. Amazon KDP requires you to disclose AI-generated text, images or translations in every book you publish or update, whether the tool that wrote it cost anything or not. Skip the disclosure and Amazon can pull the listing or suspend the account, no warning required. A free draft doesn't come with that checkbox filled in for you.

## Where the math actually lands

Run the comparison on a 15,000 to 20,000-word nonfiction book, the size most lead magnets and course companions need. On credit-metered tools you're paying **$9 to $29/month** in recurring fees until the draft is done, then adding **$147 to $1,000** for formatting and **$50 to $500** for a cover before you have a sellable file, plus your own time reconciling AI output between the writing tool and the formatting tool. None of that counts the hours spent moving a manuscript out of a text-only editor and into a layout tool by hand, checking that headings, page breaks and a table of contents survived the move, which is exactly the busywork a one-time-payment pipeline is built to skip.

[InkMagnet](/) runs research, writing, typesetting, cover and PDF+EPUB export as one pipeline, priced once per book instead of per month: **$9.99** for a Compact 30 to 45 page book up to **$34.99** for a 161 to 200 page Complete edition, with full commercial rights and no watermark on every tier, including the cheapest one. Unlimited edits and recompiles are included, so there's no meter to watch while you revise. For the same 15,000 to 20,000-word book that costs $9 to $29/month in credits before formatting even starts, the one-time price beats the "free" tool's real total cost by the time both books are actually finished.

None of this makes the free tools worthless. Sampling a model's writing voice on Rytr's free plan or testing Sudowrite's prose engine before committing to a subscription is a reasonable way to shop. Just don't expect "free" to mean "book." The credit cap, the missing export format, or the formatting bill waiting on the other side is the part every landing page leaves out of the headline.
