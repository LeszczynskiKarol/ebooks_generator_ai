Ask someone how an AI book generator actually works and most answers stop at "you type a topic and it writes the book." That description is also technically true of a chatbot, and a chatbot does not hand you a 120-page book with a working table of contents, consistent terminology and a designed cover. The pipeline behind an InkMagnet book runs four steps, and the two in the middle are the ones a plain chat window skips entirely.

## Step 1: describe the book, not the content

The first screen only asks for the shape of the decision: topic, audience, language, and a target length picked from one of five page ranges, from a 30-45 page Compact book at $9.99 up to a 161-200 page Complete book at $34.99. You also pick a visual style for the cover and any chapter illustrations. A title is optional; InkMagnet proposes one from the topic if you haven't settled on one yet. Nothing gets written at this stage. The point is to fix the constraints before a single sentence exists, for the same reason a contractor asks for square footage and budget before sketching a floor plan rather than after.

## Step 2: the structure gets approved before it gets written

This is the step raw chatbot output skips, and it's a big part of why [asking ChatGPT to write a book](/blog/write-a-book-with-chatgpt/) tends to drift by chapter nine. Once you submit a topic, the engine [researches it on the live web first](/blog/ai-books-read-like-blog-posts/) and only then drafts a chapter-by-chapter outline grounded in what it actually found. That outline isn't a five-bullet table of contents; each chapter comes with a short synopsis of the argument or content it will carry, so you can tell from the outline alone whether the book is heading somewhere useful. You can edit chapter titles and scope or approve it as drafted, and it's ready for that review within minutes of submitting the topic.

Traditional nonfiction publishing runs a version of the same check, for a different reason. [Jane Friedman](https://janefriedman.com/start-here-how-to-write-a-book-proposal/), who spent fifteen years acquiring books for traditional publishers, has written that for most nonfiction ideas you prepare a book proposal instead of a manuscript. Publishers acquire on the strength of that proposal, and the chapter-by-chapter outline inside it functions as the proof of concept for the entire book before a publisher commits an advance. An industry that puts real money behind these decisions has never trusted "trust me, it comes together" prose. It reads the outline first, every time. InkMagnet asks you to do the same thing, just before you've spent a few dollars instead of before someone else has spent tens of thousands.

## Step 3: every chapter gets written, then checked against the whole book

Once the structure is approved, each chapter gets written, then reviewed, not just for typos but for whether it still agrees with chapter three's terminology, chapter one's stated promise and the argument the outline committed to. Professional book editing separates [developmental editing from copyediting from proofreading](https://www.ka-writing.com/the-different-types-of-book-editing/) for exactly this reason: broad structural problems get caught while a chapter is still cheap to rewrite, well before anyone spends time polishing a sentence that might get cut entirely. The per-chapter review inside InkMagnet plays that developmental role while the book is still being assembled chapter by chapter, which is a much cheaper place to fix a consistency problem than discovering it on page 140 after all 200 pages already exist. Optional AI illustrations are generated and matched to each chapter's actual content at this same stage, not selected from a generic stock set and dropped in afterward.

## Step 4: download something that was never a document

The output is a press-ready PDF and a store-ready EPUB with a designed cover, compiled through the same [LaTeX typesetting pipeline](/blog/what-latex-does-for-your-book/) that handles proper hyphenation, a clickable table of contents, drop caps and styled tables automatically. There's no export step where a Word file gets reformatted to look like a book, because a Word file never existed in the first place; the chapters were structured as a book from step two onward. If you want to change a paragraph after the fact, the built-in WYSIWYG editor lets you rewrite any chapter and recompile the whole book without losing anything else you've already edited by hand. The pipeline runs once to build the first version, but nothing about the result is locked after that.

## Same order of operations, radically compressed

A traditionally published nonfiction book [typically takes 18 to 24 months](https://reedsy.com/blog/how-long-does-it-take-to-publish-a-book/) from a signed deal to a copy on shelves, and that clock starts only after a publisher has already approved a proposal and outline like the one described in step two. The speed isn't really the interesting part. What's interesting is that the steps which keep a book coherent, instead of drifting the way an unstructured chat session drifts by chapter nine, are the same steps a $9.99 book and a traditionally published one both go through.
